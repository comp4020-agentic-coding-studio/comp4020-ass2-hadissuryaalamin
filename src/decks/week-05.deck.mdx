# Spices, aromatics and oil

SLOP1640 — week 5

Marisol Quaye

---

## An aromatic is a preparation decision

An aromatic is chosen for what it changes about a dish before any heat is
applied — that makes it a preparation question, not a seasoning
afterthought.

- galangal, shallot, onion and garlic form the aromatic base of an
  enormous range of cooking traditions
- how each is cut — chopped, crushed or sliced — changes the result, and
  the difference is mechanical, not a matter of taste
- leaf aromatics answer a different question: not how they are cut, but
  when they meet heat
- oil is the third material this week treats as a preparation choice: a
  smoke point that is not fixed, and degrades with age

# Aromatics: allium first

---

## What intact tissue keeps apart

Allium tissue — shallot, onion, garlic — keeps a flavourless precursor
compound and the enzyme that acts on it in separate cell compartments.

- intact: precursor and enzyme never meet
- cut, crushed or bruised: the cell wall breaks and the two compartments
  mix
- the reaction that follows produces the pungent smell and flavour
  associated with the raw vegetable

Nothing about this depends on taste perception. It is a cell-biology
event that happens whether or not anyone is nearby to smell it.

---

## Garlic: alliinase meets alliin

Cutting or crushing garlic ruptures cells and brings the enzyme
alliinase into contact with its substrate, alliin. The product is
allicin — the compound responsible for crushed garlic's characteristic
smell.

[Borlinghaus et al. (2021), reviewing this mechanism in
*Molecules*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8001868/), traces
the reaction from intact clove to the volatile product in detail.

---

## Onion: a related but distinct pathway

Onion shares the precursor-and-enzyme structure but adds a second step.
A separate enzyme, lachrymatory factor synthase, converts one of
alliinase's reaction products into the volatile compound that makes eyes
water.

This pathway was first identified by [Imai et al. (2002) in
*Nature*](https://www.nature.com/articles/419685a). It is why cutting an
onion has a distinctive effect that cutting garlic does not, despite both
starting from the same allium chemistry.

---

## Chopped, crushed, sliced: why they differ

| Technique | Cell rupture per gram | Reaction speed |
|---|---|---|
| Slice | least | slowest, mildest |
| Chop | moderate | intermediate |
| Crush | most | fastest, strongest |

- crushing ruptures far more cells per gram than slicing does, releasing
  more enzyme and substrate into contact at once
- a crushed clove of garlic smells sharper, sooner, than a sliced one of
  the same mass — a direct mechanical consequence, not a stronger clove
- a larger cut surface also lets subsequent heat reach more of the
  material at once, so "how finely" and "how much reaction has already
  happened" are the same decision

# Leaf aromatics: timing and heat

---

## A different question from allium

Bay leaf, lime leaf, oregano and basil are not prepared by rupture in
the way alliums are. Their flavour comes from volatile aromatic
compounds that are themselves heat-sensitive — the decision that matters
is not how they are cut, but when they meet heat.

[A study of basil subjected to different heat
treatments](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8746197/) found the
volatile compound profile changed measurably with heat exposure, and
that milder treatment preserved more of the aroma associated with fresh
basil.

---

## Early or late, and why

- **bay leaf** — added early: its flavour compounds need time and heat
  to diffuse into a dish, and are robust enough to survive the exposure
- **lime leaf** — usually added early to mid-cook, for the same
  diffusion reason
- **oregano** — tolerates moderate cooking time without collapsing
- **basil** — added at the end, or off the heat entirely: its more
  delicate volatile compounds do not survive the same exposure without
  losing what made them worth adding

The timing follows from what heat does to the compound, not from
habit.

# Oil: smoke point

---

## Not a fixed constant

Every oil has a smoke point — the temperature at which it begins to
visibly break down and smoke. Reported values vary noticeably between
sources, because smoke point is a property of a specific batch of oil,
not a fixed constant of the oil type.

- the same oil, fresh versus already used, does not share a smoke point
- storage condition and age move the number before any cooking happens
- a listed figure is a starting estimate, not a guarantee for the bottle
  in hand

---

## How it degrades with use

[Crosby (2018), writing in the Institute of Food Technologists' *Food
Technology*
magazine](https://www.ift.org/news-and-publications/food-technology-magazine/issues/2018/may/features/do-cooking-oils-present-a-health-risk),
cites research finding that the formation of as little as 1% free fatty
acid in soybean oil causes a **25% decline** in its smoke point.

An oil that has already been heated, or stored badly, or kept too long,
smokes at a markedly lower temperature than the same oil fresh — the
smoke point is a moving target across an oil's working life, not a label
value.

---

## When the choice matters

Oil choice matters more as intended heat increases and matters
comparatively little where heat is gentle or absent.

- a flavourful, low-smoke-point oil used cold or barely warmed loses
  nothing by having a low smoke point, since that ceiling is never
  approached
- the same oil pushed to a high, sustained heat is the situation where
  the 1% free-fatty-acid decline actually costs something

# Before this week's tutorial

---

## Two materials, one clock

This week's tutorial exercise is a storage-duration calculation, not a
cutting exercise.

- an aromatic once cut behaves like any other cut produce for the
  purpose of a cold-chain clock — the cutting decisions above do not
  reset that clock, they precede it
- an oil's useful life is itself a storage question: how long it keeps
  its smoke point before that 1% free-fatty-acid threshold is crossed
- this lecture supplies the reason those clocks run the way they do; the
  tutorial runs the clock itself, against a stated prep date and time

Open the storage-duration calculator on this week's session page to work
through it.
