Week 2 separated produce into ethylene producers and ethylene-sensitive
produce and stored them apart on that basis. This week returns to the same
chemistry from the other side: most of the fruit responsible for that
ethylene, examined for what it is doing and when.

## Varieties and what distinguishes them

Fruit divides into two ripening behaviours, and the difference matters more
than the difference between any two species. Climacteric fruit — apples,
bananas, avocados, peaches and others — keeps producing ethylene and
respiring faster after it is picked, which is why it continues to ripen off
the plant. Non-climacteric fruit — citrus, grapes, cherries, berries — does
not get that second wind: once picked, it is as ripe as it will ever be.

## Storage: where, and why

The climacteric/non-climacteric split, not species, decides where a fruit
belongs. Bananas, citrus, mangoes, melons, papaya and pineapple are harmed
rather than helped by cold — refrigeration slows or blocks the ripening
these fruits still have left to do, so they keep to the counter. Stone
fruit, kiwi and avocado ripen on the counter first, then move to the
refrigerator once ready. Berries, cherries and grapes are non-climacteric
and were as good as they will get at harvest, so refrigeration from the
start only slows their decline.

## Choosing well at the market

Ripeness at the point of sale is read fruit by fruit rather than by one
rule: firm and unbruised for an apple, fragrant and heavy for its size for
citrus, yielding to gentle pressure for an avocado or a papaya.

## Ripening as a schedulable process

Because a climacteric fruit keeps producing the hormone that ripens it
after harvest, buying one underripe is not a compromise — it is a way of
deciding, rather than discovering, when it will be ready. A non-climacteric
fruit offers no such choice: it must be bought at the ripeness it will be
eaten at, because none is coming later.

## Apple, banana, orange, strawberry and mango: fat, protein and calories per 100 g

The invent-a-recipe assessment (week 6) asks for a nutritional composition
traceable to a named food-composition database; fruit is a common component
of that composition and deserves the same treatment as this course has
already given vegetables and meat. Figures are per 100 g raw, from [USDA
FoodData Central](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/), for the five fruits this
week's tutorial exercise draws on: the climacteric fruits here — apple,
banana, mango — and the two non-climacteric fruits — orange and
strawberry.

| Fruit | Calories (kcal) | Fat (g) | Protein (g) | FDC ID |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Apple, raw, with skin | 52 | 0.17 | 0.26 | 171688 |
| Banana, raw | 89 | 0.33 | 1.09 | 173944 |
| Orange, raw | 47 | 0.12 | 0.94 | 169097 |
| Strawberries, raw | 32 | 0.30 | 0.67 | 167762 |
| Mangos, raw | 60 | 0.38 | 0.82 | 169910 |

The calorie spread across the five tracks sugar content more than
anything else discussed this week; none of the five is a meaningful
source of fat or protein, which is worth stating so a recipe's protein
total is not accidentally attributed to its fruit component. This week's
tutorial exercise uses the macro calculator on all five of these figures,
drawn directly from FoodData Central.
