An aromatic is chosen for what it changes about a dish before any heat is
applied — a decision this week treats as a preparation question, not a
seasoning afterthought. Galangal, shallot, onion and garlic form the
aromatic base of an enormous range of cooking traditions, and the allium
members of that group share a mechanism worth understanding before any of
them are touched with a knife.

## Aromatics: allium first

Intact allium tissue keeps a flavourless precursor compound and the enzyme
that acts on it in separate cell compartments. Only when the cell wall is
broken do the two meet, and the reaction that follows produces the
pungent, characteristic smell and flavour associated with the raw
vegetable. This is documented for garlic specifically: cutting or crushing
ruptures cells and brings the enzyme alliinase into contact with its
substrate, alliin, producing allicin — a mechanism reviewed in detail by
[Borlinghaus et al. (2021) in
*Molecules*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8001868/). Onion works
by a related but distinct pathway, identified by [Imai et al. (2002) in
*Nature*](https://www.nature.com/articles/419685a).

**Crushing ruptures far more cells per gram than slicing does**, releasing
more enzyme and substrate into contact and producing a stronger, faster
reaction — which is why a crushed clove of garlic smells sharper, sooner,
than a sliced one of the same size. Chopping sits between the two.

## Bay leaf, lime leaf, oregano, basil: timing and heat

Leaf aromatics are mostly a matter of timing: their flavour comes from
volatile compounds that are themselves heat-sensitive. A [study of basil
subjected to different heat
treatments](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8746197/) found the
volatile compound profile changed measurably with heat exposure, and that
milder treatment preserved more of the aroma associated with fresh basil.
Bay leaf is added at the start because its flavour compounds need time and
heat to diffuse into a dish and are robust enough to survive the exposure;
basil is added at the end, or off the heat entirely.

## Oils: smoke point and when it matters

Every oil has a smoke point, and reported values vary between sources
because it is a property of a specific batch of oil, not a fixed constant
of the oil type. It falls over the life of the oil: [Crosby (2018), writing
in the Institute of Food Technologists' *Food Technology*
magazine](https://www.ift.org/news-and-publications/food-technology-magazine/issues/2018/may/features/do-cooking-oils-present-a-health-risk),
cites research finding that the formation of as little as 1% free fatty
acid in soybean oil causes a 25% decline in its smoke point — meaning an
oil that has already been heated, or stored badly, or kept too long, smokes
at a markedly lower temperature than the same oil fresh. Oil choice matters
more as intended heat increases and matters comparatively little where heat
is gentle or absent.

## Why this sits before the tutorial

This week's tutorial exercise is a storage-duration calculation, not a
cutting exercise: an aromatic once cut behaves like any other cut produce
for the purpose of a cold-chain clock, and an oil's useful life is itself a
storage question — how long it keeps its smoke point before that 1% free
fatty acid threshold is crossed. The lecture supplies the reason those
clocks run the way they do; the tutorial runs the clock itself.
