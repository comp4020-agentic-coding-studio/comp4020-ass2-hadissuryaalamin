## The claim

A meal is decided before it is cooked. Selection, storage, temperature,
cutting and sequence determine what arrives at the pan; the stove only
reveals a decision already made. This course spends weeks 1 through 10 on
that decision. Weeks 11 and 12 apply heat, and only to test it: the same
dishes, cooked the same way, from material that was prepared differently in
the weeks before.

## What the data show

The Bureau of Labor Statistics' American Time Use Survey, in its 2014 Eating
and Health Module, put average daily time on food preparation, serving and
cleanup at 37 minutes for Americans aged 18 and over. A separate USDA
Economic Research Service analysis, tracking the narrower activity of
preparation alone, found it rose from 23.4 minutes a day in 2004–07 to 27.5
minutes in 2014–17 — an 18 percent increase — while the share of people who
prepared food on a given day rose from 47 to 54 percent over the same
period. Feeding oneself is not a shrinking task; it is, on the aggregate
figures, a slightly growing one.

Monsivais, Aggarwal and Drewnowski surveyed 1,319 adults in 2008–09 and
grouped them by daily time spent on food preparation and cleanup: under one
hour, one to two hours, and over two hours. The groups that spent more time
preparing food reported eating more fruit and vegetables; the group that
spent the least time reported more reliance on convenience food and more
frequent eating out. The study does not show that time causes the
difference — people who prioritise convenience may simply do both things,
spend less time and eat more convenience food, for the same underlying
reason — but the association between preparation time and diet quality is
the one this course treats as worth taking seriously.

None of this is a claim about money. A defensible figure for the cost
difference between eating out and preparing at home did not turn up a
source better than marketing copy, so the course does not make that claim;
the time figures above stand on their own.

## Why the course is a bet on preparation

Time is a constraint here, not the subject — this is not a course about
doing things faster. The bet is narrower: that the 10 weeks before the stove
determine more of the outcome than the time spent at the stove itself, and
that this is checkable. Weeks 11 and 12 check it directly, by holding the
cooking constant and varying only what happened before.
