This week is taught by a guest: a working head chef, not a member of the
regular teaching staff. The material is the same preparation this course has
argued for since week 1, now observed under a constraint the course itself
cannot impose — a ticket that must leave the pass in a stated number of
minutes, repeated for hours, with no allowance for a slow afternoon.

Nothing in this lecture is cooked in front of the class. The subject is the
schedule that precedes the heat: how a professional kitchen decides, in
advance, what preparation happens before service and what does not.

## Outline

**Before doors open.** What is finished to completion ahead of time, because
its quality does not depend on how recently it was made — stocks, cured or
brined items, anything improved by resting. This is preparation in its purest
form: work done when no one is waiting for it.

**Held.** What is brought to a stable, safe intermediate state and kept there
under controlled temperature until called — portioned, blanched, staged —
rather than finished, because finishing early would cost it the quality
finishing late would also cost.

**Made to order.** What is deliberately left undone until the ticket arrives,
because any earlier finishing point degrades it faster than the wait for the
order does. The decision is not "what can be prepared ahead" but "what is
harmed less by waiting unfinished than by waiting finished."

**Sequencing.** How the order of work across a service is decided once the
first two categories are fixed: which stations depend on which others,
where a bottleneck forms if two slow items land on the same ticket, and how
that is planned for rather than discovered mid-service.

## Why this week sits here

Week 1 made the case for preparation with a claim about time: most of it, for
most people, is already spent before any heat is applied, so that is where
attention pays off. This week does not add a new argument. It takes the same
claim into a setting where getting the order of preparation wrong is not an
inconvenience but a missed ticket, and shows the same three categories —
finish now, hold, or wait — doing the work under pressure that week 1 only
had room to state as time budgets on a page.

The distinction from weeks 11 and 12 is deliberate: this lecture describes
how a kitchen times its preparation. It is not itself a cooking session, and
no dish is made to order in this room.
