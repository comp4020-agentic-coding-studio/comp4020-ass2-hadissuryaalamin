Chicken and fish are the primary material this week; duck is touched on
briefly as a point of contrast. The order is deliberate: anatomy first, so
that the reason a cut behaves the way it does is visible before any
handling instruction is given.

## Anatomy

A bird's leg and thigh work constantly — walking, standing, bearing weight —
and carry more connective tissue and a higher proportion of the
slow-twitch, myoglobin-rich fibre commonly called dark meat. The breast, in
a bird that does not fly far, does comparatively little work and is
correspondingly leaner and paler. Fat sits mostly under the skin rather than
running through the muscle. Duck differs mainly in degree: a bird bred for
sustained flight or cold-water swimming carries a thicker subcutaneous fat
layer and darker meat across the whole carcass, including the breast.

Fish have no need to support their own weight against gravity in the way a
land animal does, so the connective tissue is thinner and the muscle is
arranged in short segments (myotomes) rather than long fibres. This is why
fish flakes apart along clean lines under far less force than poultry or
red meat, and why fillets are portioned along those natural seams rather
than cut across them.

## Handling

Anatomy explains what a cut can do; handling determines whether it is still
safe to do it by the time it reaches a pan. Three things matter in
sequence:

- **Cold chain.** Raw poultry and raw fish are both perishable enough that
  the two-hour rule applies: never left out of refrigeration for more than
  two hours, or more than one hour above 32°C. This is the same danger-zone
  logic that governs every perishable protein in this course — bacteria
  common to raw poultry and seafood grow fastest between 4°C and 60°C, and
  can double in as little as twenty minutes within that band.
- **Cross-contamination.** A cutting board and knife used on raw poultry or
  fish should not touch produce or anything already cooked without being
  washed first — hot, soapy water, then a clean rinse and a full dry (or a
  dishwasher cycle, for a nonporous board). The point is not fastidiousness;
  it is that the same organisms that make raw poultry and fish perishable
  transfer readily to whatever the board touches next.
- **Portioning.** Poultry is broken down along the joints the anatomy
  already defines — separating where connective tissue is thinnest keeps
  the operation clean and avoids splintering bone. Fish is portioned along
  the myotome seams and away from the pin bones, for the same reason: work
  with the structure rather than across it.

## Storage and safe holding times

Fresh raw poultry, refrigerated at 4.4°C (40°F) or below, is good for one to
two days before it should be cooked or frozen; frozen at −17.8°C (0°F) or
below, it is safe indefinitely, though quality falls off after roughly nine
months for parts and a year for a whole bird. Raw fish and shellfish follow
the same one-to-two-day window under refrigeration, and should go on ice or
into the fridge within two hours of purchase — one hour if the ambient
temperature is above 32°C.

These are food-safety limits, not cooking instructions: the material in
this course is stored, sorted and portioned, and the storage clock is what
this week is teaching a student to read.

## Fat, protein, calories and iron per 100 g

The invent-a-recipe assessment later in the semester asks for a
nutritional composition traceable to a named food-composition database;
this week's four materials — the same four the tutorial's macro
calculator offers — are as good a place as any to start reading one.
Figures are per 100 g raw, from [USDA FoodData
Central](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/).

| Material | Calories (kcal) | Fat (g) | Protein (g) | Iron (mg) | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chicken breast, meat only, raw | 120 | 2.62 | 22.5 | 0.37 | FDC ID 171077 |
| Salmon, Atlantic, farmed, raw | 208 | 13.42 | 20.42 | 0.34 | FDC ID 175167 |
| Cod, Atlantic, raw | 82 | 0.67 | 17.81 | 0.38 | FDC ID 171955 |
| Duck, meat only, raw | 135 | 5.95 | 18.28 | 2.40 | FDC ID 172410 |

Salmon's higher fat figure is consistent with the anatomy above: a
cold-water swimmer stores more fat through its flesh than a bird whose
breast, in particular, was bred to do comparatively little work. The
iron column follows the same dark-meat/white-meat split already drawn
for poultry: duck, dark meat across the whole carcass, carries more than
six times chicken breast's iron.
