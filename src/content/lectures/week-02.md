A vegetable's life after purchase is mostly decided by where it goes next,
not by anything done to it at the stove — this week's material sits entirely
inside weeks 1 through 10's preparation-only remit. Two mistakes are common
and opposite: refrigerating something that does not tolerate cold, and
leaving out something that spoils quickly at room temperature.

## Families, briefly

Vegetables are grouped here by what they have in common rather than by
species: roots and tubers (potato, carrot), alliums (onion, already
touched on in outline only — week 5 covers what cutting does to them),
leafy greens (spinach, lettuce) and brassicas (broccoli, cauliflower,
Brussels sprouts). The grouping matters because storage behaviour tracks
the family more reliably than it tracks any single vegetable's popularity.
Cuts are noted only as they affect keeping quality this week — the knife
has its own week, week 7, and this lecture does not pre-empt it.

## Storage: shelf, drawer, cold or not

Potatoes, sweet potatoes, whole winter squash and dry onions keep best
somewhere cool and dark but not refrigerated — cold temperatures convert
starch to sugar in a potato and shorten rather than extend its useful life.
Leafy greens, most brassicas, carrots and herbs go into the refrigerator,
ideally in the crisper drawer. The [FDA advises keeping the refrigerator at
or below 4 °C (40 °F) and the freezer at or below −18 °C
(0 °F)](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/are-you-storing-food-safely),
checked with an appliance thermometer rather than assumed from the dial
setting.

Within the refrigerator, a high-humidity crisper setting (vent closed)
slows moisture loss and suits leafy greens; a low-humidity setting (vent
open) lets moisture and ethylene gas escape, and suits produce that would
otherwise soften faster in a damp, sealed drawer. The [University of
Arkansas Cooperative Extension Service's consumer
guidance](https://www.uaex.uada.edu/counties/miller/news/fcs/fruits-veggies/Spoiled-Rotten-How-to-Store-Fruits-and-Vegetables.aspx)
puts leafy greens, broccoli and asparagus in the high-humidity drawer.

## Ethylene: producers and the sensitive

Ethylene is a plant hormone that accelerates ripening — and, past a point,
decay — in whatever produce sits near it. The [USDA Agricultural Marketing
Service's technical report on
ethylene](https://www.ams.usda.gov/sites/default/files/media/EthyleneCropsTechnicalReport2023.pdf)
describes apples, bananas, avocados, pears, stone fruit and tomatoes as high
producers; leafy greens, broccoli, carrots and cucumbers are sensitive to it
rather than producing it. Separating the two groups — different drawers, or
at minimum not sealed together in the same bag — is the single highest-value
habit this week teaches. Week 9 returns to this same chemistry from the
fruit side of the exchange.

## Choosing well at market

Firmness, colour and smell are the three checks worth making before
anything goes in a bag: produce that is already soft, discoloured at cut
edges, or smelling faintly fermented has already started a decline that
storage can only slow, not reverse.

## Common vegetables: calories, fat, protein and fibre per 100 g

Nutritional composition is not a storage question, but this course's
invent-a-recipe assessment asks a student to reason about a dish's
composition before it is cooked, and vegetables are frequently the largest
component by weight in a prepared recipe. Figures below are per 100 g of
the raw, edible portion, drawn from [USDA FoodData
Central](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/), the same database the assessment
brief names, and grouped by the family groupings this week already uses.

| Vegetable | Calories (kcal) | Fat (g) | Protein (g) | Fibre (g) | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Potato, flesh and skin, raw | 77 | 0.10 | 2.05 | 2.10 | FDC ID 170026 |
| Carrot, raw | 41 | 0.24 | 0.93 | 2.80 | FDC ID 170393 |
| Onion, raw | 40 | 0.10 | 1.10 | 1.70 | FDC ID 170000 |
| Garlic, raw | 149 | 0.50 | 6.36 | 2.10 | FDC ID 169230 |
| Spinach, raw | 23 | 0.39 | 2.86 | 2.20 | FDC ID 168462 |
| Lettuce, cos/romaine, raw | 17 | 0.30 | 1.23 | 2.10 | FDC ID 169247 |
| Broccoli, raw | 34 | 0.37 | 2.82 | 2.60 | FDC ID 170379 |
| Cauliflower, raw | 25 | 0.28 | 1.92 | 2.00 | FDC ID 169986 |

None of the eight is a meaningful source of fat; the calorie content across
this group tracks carbohydrate and water content rather than protein or fat
— garlic's comparatively low water content is why it is the outlier at
149 kcal, worth stating plainly rather than leaving a student to assume
vegetables are interchangeable on this measure. The tutorial session's macro
calculator draws on five of these — onion, carrot, potato, spinach and
broccoli — using the same per-100 g figures shown here, scaled to whatever
quantity a recipe calls for; garlic, lettuce and cauliflower are not yet
selectable in the calculator.
