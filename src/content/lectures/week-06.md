A kitchen's non-heat tools fall into a small number of categories, each
with a job distinct from the others: a cutting board to work on, a chopper
to cut with, strainers to separate solid from liquid, and containers to
hold what has already been prepared. None of this week's content is about
technique with any of these tools — the knife itself is week 7's subject —
only about the tools and surfaces as objects that need choosing and
maintaining.

## Board material, compared honestly

Wood, plastic and marble each trade off differently across hygiene, knife
wear and particle shedding, and none of the three wins outright.

**Wood** is kind to knife edges — the surface yields slightly rather than
blunting the blade — and a well-maintained wood board is not automatically
less hygienic than plastic: wood's natural porosity can draw liquid, and
the bacteria in it, below the surface, away from a subsequent cut, though
this depends entirely on the board being dried properly. It requires more
upkeep: periodic oiling, and it cannot go in a dishwasher.

**Plastic** is dishwasher-safe, cheap to replace, and takes knife scarring
readily — visible cut marks accumulate and, once deep enough, are harder to
clean thoroughly than a smooth surface. It is gentle on edges, similar to
wood.

**Marble and other hard stone** boards are the hardest of the three, so
they are the least forgiving to a knife edge, but they shed comparatively
little material of their own; they are heavy, can chip, and are mostly
chosen for pastry work rather than as a general-purpose board.

## What cutting actually does to the board — and the food

Every board sheds some material into whatever is chopped on it. [Yadav et
al. (2023), in *Environmental Science &
Technology*](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/abs/10.1021/acs.est.3c00924), measured
microplastic release from polyethylene and polypropylene cutting boards
during normal chopping and found a substantial number of microplastic
particles could be generated and transferred to food this way. Reporting
this finding honestly means reporting its limits along with its headline:
the same study tested the released particles against cultured mouse
fibroblast cells over 72 hours and found no significant effect on cell
viability at the concentrations tested, and the authors called for further
research rather than treating the question as closed. Both halves of that
sentence are part of the finding.

## Maintenance

Strainers and containers are maintained by the same basic rule as boards:
wash promptly, dry fully before storing, and replace anything whose surface
has degraded to the point of trapping food or liquid. For a wood board
specifically: wash with hot soapy water rather than soaking, dry standing
on edge, and oil periodically with a food-safe mineral or specialist board
oil.

## Why this sits before the knife

A knife is only as good as the surface it works against. Week 7 covers
blade geometry, sharpening and cuts in detail; this week's board and tool
choices set the surface those cuts land on, and a board that dulls an edge
faster than it needs to is a preparation cost paid every time it is used,
not once.
