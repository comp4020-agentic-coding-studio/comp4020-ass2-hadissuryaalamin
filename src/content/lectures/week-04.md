Last week's structure repeats here on purpose: anatomy first, then
handling, then portioning. Beef is the primary material because it is the
animal most students will have handled in the widest range of cuts; goat
and lamb are touched on briefly as the same argument applied to a smaller
frame with a different fat distribution, not as a separate topic.

## Anatomy: why some cuts are tough and some are not

A cut's texture is a direct record of what the muscle did in the living
animal. Muscles that worked constantly against load — the leg, shoulder and
neck — build dense muscle fibre wrapped in a high proportion of collagen,
the connective tissue protein that makes an unrendered cut chewy. Muscles
that did comparatively little work — the loin, along the animal's back —
have far less connective tissue and a finer grain, which is why they are
tender with minimal treatment and dry out rather than soften if treated the
way a shoulder is treated. Fat distribution follows a related but separate
logic: it accumulates where the animal stored energy, not where it worked
hardest. Goat and lamb carry proportionally less external fat than beef of
a comparable cut, and their smaller muscle groups mean connective tissue is
a larger share of any given cut's mass.

**Which cuts suit which treatment follows from this directly, not from
convention.** A cut high in collagen needs time and moisture for that
collagen to convert to gelatin; a cut with little collagen gains nothing
from the time a tougher cut requires. Matching treatment to cut is
therefore a question with an anatomical answer, even though tradition
usually arrives at the same answer by trial rather than by dissection.

## Handling and portioning

The handling rules from week 3 hold here without amendment: refrigerate raw
meat promptly (within 2 hours, 1 hour above 32 °C / 90 °F), keep it at or
below 4 °C, and treat any surface or tool that has touched raw meat as
contaminated until washed. Portioning along, rather than across, the
direction of connective tissue and fibre is what makes a portion cook
predictably.

## Storage windows

[USDA FSIS
guidance](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/steps-keep-food-safe)
gives raw beef, veal, lamb and pork a refrigerated window of **3 to 5 days**
before it should be cooked or frozen — longer than the 2-day window for
poultry, fish and ground meats covered last week. Ground and minced red
meat is the exception: grinding mixes surface bacteria through the whole
volume, so it follows the same **2-day** window as poultry and fish rather
than the longer whole-cut window. The [Danger
Zone](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/danger-zone-40f-140f)
(4 °C to 60 °C) applies identically to red meat as to poultry and fish.

## Two beef blends, lamb and goat: fat, protein, calories and iron per 100 g

The two beef entries below are ground (minced) raw meat, because that is
the form this course's invent-a-recipe assessment most often reaches for,
and minced meat carries the shortest safe holding window discussed above
— a reason to compute a dish's nutritional composition against the exact
cut named, not against "beef" in general. Lamb and goat are given as
whole raw cuts, the lower-fat comparison point the calculator sets
against the two beef blends. Figures are per 100 g raw, from
[USDA FoodData Central](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/).

| Cut | Calories (kcal) | Fat (g) | Protein (g) | Iron (mg) | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Beef, ground, 85% lean / 15% fat, raw | 215 | 15.0 | 18.59 | 2.09 | FDC ID 171796 |
| Beef, ground, 93% lean / 7% fat, raw | 152 | 7.0 | 20.85 | 2.33 | FDC ID 173110 |
| Lamb, domestic, leg, whole, raw | 230 | 17.2 | 17.91 | 1.66 | FDC ID 174311 |
| Goat, raw | 109 | 2.3 | 20.6 | 2.83 | FDC ID 175303 |

Fat is where the four separate most clearly, from 2.3 g in goat to 17.2 g
in the whole lamb leg — a wider spread than the two beef blends show
against each other. Iron does not track fat the same way: goat, the
leanest of the four, also carries the most iron per 100 g, and lamb leg,
the fattiest, carries the least. Which of these a recipe should reach for
is still the anatomy question discussed above, not one a nutrition table
settles.
