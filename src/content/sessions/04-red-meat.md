## The same argument, a second material

Last week's structure repeats here on purpose: anatomy first, then handling,
then portioning. Beef is the primary material because it is the animal most
students will have handled in the widest range of cuts; goat and lamb are
touched on briefly as the same argument applied to a smaller frame with a
different fat distribution, not as a separate topic.

## Anatomy: why some cuts are tough and some are not

A cut's texture is a direct record of what the muscle did in the living
animal. Muscles that worked constantly against load — the leg, shoulder and
neck — build dense, well-exercised muscle fibre wrapped in a high proportion
of collagen, the connective tissue protein that makes an unrendered cut
chewy. Muscles that did comparatively little work — the loin, along the
animal's back — have far less connective tissue and a finer grain, which is
why they are tender with minimal treatment and why they dry out rather than
soften if treated the way a shoulder is treated. Fat distribution follows a
related but separate logic: it accumulates where the animal stored energy
(around the loin and rump, and, breed-dependent, marbled through the
muscle) rather than where it worked hardest. Goat and lamb carry
proportionally less external fat than beef of a comparable cut, and the
smaller muscle groups mean connective tissue is a larger share of any given
cut's total mass — one reason recipes calling for slow, moist cooking of
lamb shoulder or shank exist across many culinary traditions independently:
the same anatomy produces the same conclusion regardless of who is drawing
it.

**Which cuts suit which treatment follows from this directly, not from
convention.** A cut high in collagen needs time and moisture for that
collagen to convert to gelatin; a cut with little collagen needs neither, and
gains nothing from the time a tougher cut requires. Matching treatment to
cut is therefore a question with an anatomical answer, even though tradition
usually arrives at the same answer by trial rather than by dissection.

## Handling and portioning

The handling rules from week 3 hold here without amendment: refrigerate
raw meat promptly (within 2 hours, 1 hour above 32 °C / 90 °F), keep it at or
below 4 °C, and treat any surface or tool that has touched raw meat as
contaminated until it is washed. Portioning — dividing a larger cut into
the pieces a given treatment calls for — is where the anatomical argument
becomes practical: portioning along, rather than across, the direction of
connective tissue and fibre is what makes a portion cook predictably: cut
against that logic and two visually similar pieces from the same cut can
behave completely differently under identical treatment.

## Storage windows

[USDA FSIS
guidance](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/steps-keep-food-safe)
gives raw beef, veal, lamb and pork a refrigerated window of **3 to 5 days**
before it should be cooked or frozen — longer than the 2-day window for
poultry, fish and ground meats covered last week, consistent with the lower
surface-area-to-mass ratio and generally lower moisture content of whole
red-meat cuts. Ground and minced red meat is the exception: because grinding
mixes surface bacteria through the whole volume, it follows the same
**2-day** window as poultry and fish rather than the longer whole-cut
window, and this is worth stating explicitly since it is the most common
place the rule is applied incorrectly. The [Danger
Zone](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/danger-zone-40f-140f)
(4 °C to 60 °C) applies identically to red meat as to poultry and fish: it is
a property of bacterial growth rates, not of the animal.
