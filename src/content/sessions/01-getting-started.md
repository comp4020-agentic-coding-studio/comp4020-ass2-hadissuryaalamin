## The thesis

The course is built on one claim, stated here so it can be checked later: **a
meal is decided before it is cooked.** Selection, storage, temperature,
cutting and sequence set the outcome; heat only reveals what was already
decided. Nothing taught in weeks 1 through 10 involves a stove, because none
of it needs one — the argument is that most of what determines whether a meal
turns out well has already happened by the time one is lit.

This is not a claim that preparation is more *important* than cooking. It is
a narrower claim: that preparation and cooking are separable, and that the
separable part is worth a semester of attention on its own. The thesis will
be tested directly in weeks 11 and 12, where identical dishes are made from
differently prepared material so that any difference in the result can be
attributed to the preparation rather than to the cooking.

## What preparing actually costs

The case for taking preparation seriously starts with what it already costs,
measured rather than guessed. The [US Bureau of Labor Statistics' American
Time Use Survey, reported via USDA's Economic Research
Service](https://www.ers.usda.gov/amber-waves/2016/november/americans-spend-an-average-of-37-minutes-a-day-preparing-and-serving-food-and-cleaning-up),
found that Americans spend an average of 37 minutes a day on food
preparation, serving and clean-up. A [follow-up ERS
report](https://www.ers.usda.gov/amber-waves/2020/april/more-americans-spend-more-time-in-food-related-activities-than-a-decade-ago)
found that figure has risen over the following decade, not fallen — the
common assumption that convenience food is steadily replacing home
preparation does not hold up against the survey data.

Time spent is not neutral with respect to outcome. [Monsivais, Aggarwal and
Drewnowski
(2014)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0749379714004000),
writing in the *American Journal of Preventive Medicine*, examined how time
spent on home food preparation relates to indicators of healthy eating, and
found more time in preparation associated with a more favourable dietary
pattern. The course does not treat this as license to assume more time is
always better — only as evidence that how a meal is prepared is not a
side-issue to whether it is a good one.

Money moves alongside time. The [Bureau of Labor Statistics' Consumer
Expenditures report for
2024](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cesan.nr0.htm) records average annual
household spending of $6,224 on food at home against $3,945 on food away from
home — food prepared at home is not the cheap option by default, it is the
option where the household absorbs the labour instead of paying someone else
to do it. Eating quickly and eating well are not opposites; they are
reconciled before the stove is turned on, or not at all.

## Why the semester ends the way it does

Weeks 1 through 10 are preparation, in the narrow sense used all semester:
selecting, storing, handling, cutting and combining, with no cooking step.
Week 11 puts identical prepared material through the same cooking method so
the preparation's contribution is visible on its own; week 12 is a live,
assessed practical under time pressure. Everything between here and then is
building the case, and the material, for that test.
