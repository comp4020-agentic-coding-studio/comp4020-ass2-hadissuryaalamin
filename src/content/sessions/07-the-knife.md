The lecture describes a knife. This session is where one is used. Nothing
here is cooked — a knife's job this week is to turn a whole piece of
material into pieces of a stated size and shape, for storage or for a later
step, and the session checks that the piece can do that job, not what
happens to the pieces afterwards.

## Before the session

Bring a knife if you have one you already trust; a bench set is available
for anyone who does not. Either way, weigh it in your hand before you start.
A maker's own specification sheet for a standard 200 mm chef's knife lists a
weight of 265 g and a factory edge of roughly 14 degrees; a knife that feels
much lighter or much heavier than that changes where its balance point sits,
and therefore how it should be held.

## In the session

**Weigh-in.** Compare the bench knife's blade length, weight and edge
against the reference figures from the deck. Western-style kitchen knives
are typically sharpened around 20–22 degrees per side and run 52–58 on the
Rockwell hardness scale; harder blades hold an edge longer but chip more
readily if steeled carelessly. None of this is trivia — a knife outside
these ranges behaves differently at the bench, and the grip correction that
follows depends on knowing which way it differs.

**Grip.** Pinch grip on the knife hand — thumb and the first knuckle of the
index finger gripping the blade itself just above the handle, the remaining
fingers wrapped around the handle — and claw grip on the guiding hand,
knuckles forward, fingertips tucked under and away from the blade's path.
Both are corrected individually before anyone is allowed to work at speed.

**The cuts.** The shapes named in the deck, produced at the bench rather
than watched on a slide: uniform sticks, then a uniform dice from those
sticks, then a fine ribbon cut on a leafy vegetable. Consistency of size is
what is checked, not speed — an uneven cut this week is an uneven result in
week 11, when the same material meets heat for the first time.

**Maintenance.** A honing rod is on every bench. Hone before you start:
honing realigns an edge that has rolled slightly out of true without
removing any metal, so it can be done every time a knife is picked up.
Sharpening removes metal to rebuild an edge that honing can no longer
restore, and runs on a slower cycle — roughly monthly under heavy,
professional-style use, a few times a year under home-style use. This
week's bench knives are checked and, if due, sharpened by the demonstrator;
students are not issued a stone until that distinction is second nature.

## Why there is no calculator this week

This week's exercise is at the bench, not on screen: the reference figures
for edge angle and hardness are given directly above rather than through a
calculator, because what is being checked is a knife in a student's hand
against those figures, not a value computed from a chosen ingredient.

## Afterwards

Feedback is on grip and on the consistency of the cut pieces, not on
outcome — there is no outcome yet. What a marker checks going forward is
whether an uneven or unsafe cut from this week shows up again in week 11's
comparison; if it does, that is data about preparation, which is the point
of this course.
