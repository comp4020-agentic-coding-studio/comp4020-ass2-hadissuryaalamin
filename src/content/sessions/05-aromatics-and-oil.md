## Aromatics: allium first

Galangal, shallot, onion and garlic form the aromatic base of an enormous
range of cooking traditions, and the allium members of that group (shallot,
onion, garlic) share a mechanism worth understanding before any of them are
touched with a knife. Intact allium tissue keeps a flavourless precursor
compound and the enzyme that acts on it in separate cell compartments. Only
when the cell wall is broken do the two meet, and the reaction that follows
produces the pungent, characteristic smell and flavour associated with the
raw vegetable. This is documented for garlic specifically: cutting or
crushing ruptures cells and brings the enzyme alliinase into contact with
its substrate, alliin, producing allicin — the compound responsible for
crushed garlic's smell — a mechanism reviewed in detail by [Borlinghaus et
al. (2021) in
*Molecules*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8001868/). Onion works
by a related but distinct pathway: a separate enzyme, lachrymatory factor
synthase, converts one of alliinase's reaction products into the volatile
compound that makes eyes water, a mechanism first identified by [Imai et al.
(2002) in *Nature*](https://www.nature.com/articles/419685a).

**This is why chopped, crushed and sliced alliums behave differently, and
the difference is mechanical rather than a matter of taste preference.**
Crushing ruptures far more cells per gram than slicing does, releasing more
enzyme and substrate into contact and producing a stronger, faster reaction
— which is why a crushed clove of garlic smells sharper, sooner, than a
sliced one of the same size. Chopping sits between the two: more cell
rupture than a clean slice, less than a crush. Surface area follows the same
logic in the other direction: a larger exposed surface, from a finer cut,
lets the volatile reaction products escape and lets subsequent cooking heat
reach more of the material at once, so the practical effect of "how finely"
is inseparable from "how much" of the reaction has already happened by the
time the aromatic goes anywhere near heat.

## Bay leaf, lime leaf, oregano, basil: timing and heat

Where allium aromatics are prepared by rupture, leaf aromatics — bay leaf,
lime leaf, oregano, basil — are mostly a matter of timing. Their flavour
comes from volatile aromatic compounds that are themselves heat-sensitive:
prolonged or high heat drives them off or breaks them down rather than
releasing them cleanly. A [study of basil subjected to different heat
treatments](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8746197/) found the
volatile compound profile changed measurably with heat exposure, and that
milder treatment preserved more of the aroma associated with fresh basil
than harsher treatment did. This is the practical basis for adding delicate
leaf herbs — basil, in particular — late, and tougher aromatics like bay
leaf early: bay leaf is added at the start because its flavour compounds
need time and heat to diffuse into a dish and are robust enough to survive
the exposure; basil is added at the end, or off the heat entirely, because
its more delicate compounds do not survive the same exposure without losing
what made them worth adding.

## Oils: smoke point and when it matters

Every oil has a smoke point — the temperature at which it begins to
visibly break down and smoke — and reported values vary noticeably between
sources, because the smoke point is a property of a specific batch of oil,
not a fixed constant of the oil type. It falls over the life of the oil:
[Crosby (2018), writing in the Institute of Food Technologists' *Food
Technology*
magazine](https://www.ift.org/news-and-publications/food-technology-magazine/issues/2018/may/features/do-cooking-oils-present-a-health-risk),
cites research finding that the formation of as little as 1% free fatty acid
in soybean oil causes a 25% decline in its smoke point — meaning an oil that
has already been heated, or stored badly, or kept too long, smokes at a
markedly lower temperature than the same oil fresh. This is also why more
refined oils and clarified fats (such as ghee, clarified from butter)
tolerate higher heat than their unrefined or whole counterparts: refining
and clarification remove exactly the non-fat material — free fatty acids,
milk solids, plant particulates — that breaks down first and lowers the
smoke point.

The practical consequence is that oil choice matters more as intended heat
increases and matters comparatively little where heat is gentle or absent
— a flavourful, low-smoke-point oil used cold or barely warmed loses nothing
by having a low smoke point, since that ceiling is never approached.
