## Anatomy before handling

The reason a cut of chicken or fish behaves the way it does is anatomical,
not traditional, so anatomy comes first. Muscle that does continuous, low-
intensity work — a chicken's leg and thigh, most of a fish's body — carries
more connective tissue and fat, and holds up to longer or rougher handling.
Muscle used only in short bursts — a chicken's breast, which does not fly
far — has less connective tissue, less fat, and is correspondingly less
forgiving: it dries out and toughens faster under the same treatment that
a thigh tolerates easily. Fish differ from land animals in a further way
relevant to handling: their muscle fibres are shorter, arranged in
segments (myotomes) separated by thin sheets of connective tissue, which is
part of why fish flakes rather than shreds and why it is more fragile to
handle than poultry of a comparable size. Duck sits between the two poultry
extremes discussed here — more fat under the skin than chicken, breast and
leg meat closer in character to each other than in chicken — but duck is not
this week's primary material and is not covered beyond that point.

## Handling: cold chain, cross-contamination, cleaning

Handling follows directly from anatomy in one respect: more delicate muscle
tolerates less time out of refrigeration before quality and safety both
suffer. Both poultry and fish are considered highly perishable by food
safety authorities, and the practical rules are the same for both.

**Cold chain.** Raw poultry and raw fish should go from market to
refrigerator (at or below 4 °C / 40 °F) within two hours, or within one hour
if the ambient temperature is above 32 °C (90 °F). Between 4 °C and 60 °C —
the ["Danger
Zone"](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/danger-zone-40f-140f)
— bacteria capable of causing illness can double in number in as little as
20 minutes, which is the reason the two-hour figure is not a rounded
guess but a stated limit.

**Cross-contamination.** Raw poultry and raw fish carry organisms that a
cutting board, knife or hand can transfer to anything touched next.
Dedicating a board (or at minimum thoroughly washing one) between raw
protein and anything eaten raw — vegetables, herbs, bread — is not
fastidiousness, it is the entire point of cleaning between tasks rather than
at the end of them.

**Cleaning.** Surfaces and tools that touched raw poultry or fish need
washing with hot, soapy water before they touch anything else, and again
before the next use. This session does not prescribe a sanitising regimen
in detail — the kitchen's tools and surfaces are week 6's subject — beyond
naming cleaning as a handling step that happens during preparation, not
after it.

## Storage windows and safe holding times

[USDA FSIS
guidance](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/steps-keep-food-safe)
states that fresh poultry, fish and ground meats should be cooked or frozen
within **2 days** of refrigeration. FSIS's own [answer on fish
storage](https://ask.fsis.usda.gov/article/How-long-can-you-store-fish)
narrows this further for seafood specifically: raw fish and shellfish keep
only **1 to 2 days** refrigerated at 4 °C or below before they should be
cooked or frozen; frozen raw fish is best used within 3 to 8 months, and
frozen shellfish within 3 to 12 months, for quality rather than strict
safety. These windows are shorter than the 3-to-5-day window given for red
meat next week, which is itself a consequence of the anatomy discussed
above: less connective tissue and fat generally means a shorter safe margin,
not a longer one.
