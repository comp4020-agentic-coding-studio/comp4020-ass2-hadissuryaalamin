## Storage is the first decision

A vegetable's life after purchase is mostly decided by where it goes next.
Two mistakes are common and opposite: refrigerating something that does not
tolerate cold, and leaving out something that spoils quickly at room
temperature. Potatoes, sweet potatoes, whole winter squash and dry onions
keep best somewhere cool and dark but not refrigerated — cold temperatures
change the starch in potatoes and shorten rather than extend their useful
life. Leafy greens, most brassicas (broccoli, cauliflower, Brussels sprouts),
carrots and herbs go into the refrigerator, ideally in the crisper drawer.

The reference point for the refrigerator itself is fixed: the [FDA advises
keeping the refrigerator at or below 4 °C (40 °F) and the freezer at or below
-18 °C (0
°F)](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/are-you-storing-food-safely),
checked with an appliance thermometer rather than assumed from the dial
setting. [FoodSafety.gov's Cold Food Storage
Chart](https://www.foodsafety.gov/food-safety-charts/cold-food-storage-charts)
uses the same 4 °C benchmark for how long refrigerated food keeps before
quality — not just safety — starts to decline.

## Shelf, drawer, humidity

Within the refrigerator, the crisper drawer exists because most vegetables
and fruit do not want the same humidity as the rest of the interior. A
high-humidity setting (vent closed) slows moisture loss and suits leafy
greens and other produce that wilts quickly. A low-humidity setting (vent
open) lets moisture and gas escape, and suits produce that would otherwise
soften or rot faster in a damp, sealed drawer. The [University of Arkansas
Cooperative Extension Service's consumer
guidance](https://www.uaex.uada.edu/counties/miller/news/fcs/fruits-veggies/Spoiled-Rotten-How-to-Store-Fruits-and-Vegetables.aspx)
puts leafy greens, broccoli and asparagus in the high-humidity drawer, and
apples, berries and tomatoes — all significant ethylene producers — in the
low-humidity drawer, precisely so the gas they give off has somewhere to go
rather than accumulating around more sensitive neighbours.

## Ethylene: producers and the sensitive

Ethylene is a plant hormone, released by some produce as it matures and
capable of accelerating ripening — and, past a point, decay — in whatever
sits near it. The [USDA Agricultural Marketing Service's technical report on
ethylene](https://www.ams.usda.gov/sites/default/files/media/EthyleneCropsTechnicalReport2023.pdf)
describes the gas being produced in fruit as it reaches physiological
maturity, triggering the chemical changes associated with ripening. Apples,
bananas, avocados, pears, stone fruit and tomatoes are high producers.
Leafy greens, broccoli, carrots, cucumbers and most other vegetables are
sensitive to it rather than producing it themselves, and ripen or spoil
faster when stored alongside a producer. Separating the two groups —
different drawers, or at minimum not sealed together in the same bag — is the
single highest-value habit this session teaches.

The relationship is not limited to ripening on purpose. Wounding a vegetable
— a bruise, a cut surface — measurably increases both its ethylene output and
its respiration rate, which is one reason cut produce keeps for hours where
the same vegetable whole keeps for days. This is documented across a range of
produce in the postharvest physiology literature, including a [2022 review of
ethylene management in vegetable
crops](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2022.968315/full).
The knife itself is next week's neighbour but two — this course's knife week
is week 7 — so cutting is noted here only for what it does to keeping
quality, not for how it is done.

## Choosing well at market

Buying decisions are storage decisions made early. Firmness, colour and smell
are the three checks worth making before anything goes in a bag: produce that
is already soft, discoloured at cut edges, or smelling faintly fermented has
already started the decline that storage can only slow, not reverse. Buying
less, more often, of produce that does not keep well is frequently a better
strategy than buying in bulk and hoping the crisper drawer compensates for a
week's delay.
