Week 2 separated produce into ethylene producers and ethylene-sensitive
produce and stored them apart on that basis. This week returns to the same
chemistry from the other side: most of the fruit responsible for that
ethylene, examined for what it is doing and when.

## Varieties and what distinguishes them

Fruit divides into two ripening behaviours, and the difference matters more
than the difference between any two species. Climacteric fruit — apples,
bananas, avocados, peaches and others — keeps producing ethylene and
respiring faster after it is picked, which is why it continues to ripen off
the plant. Non-climacteric fruit — citrus, grapes, cherries, berries — does
not get that second wind: once picked, it is as ripe as it will ever be.

## Storage: where, and why

The same climacteric/non-climacteric split, not species, decides where a
fruit belongs. Bananas, citrus, mangoes, melons, papaya and pineapple are
harmed rather than helped by cold — refrigeration slows or blocks the
ripening these fruits still have left to do, so they keep to the counter.
Stone fruit, kiwi and avocado are the opposite of both extremes: ripen them
on the counter first, then move them to the refrigerator for a few days
once they are ready, because refrigeration stops ripening but is exactly
what a fruit that has already finished ripening needs. Berries, cherries
and grapes are non-climacteric and were as good as they will get at
harvest, so refrigeration from the start only slows their decline rather
than interrupting anything useful.

## Choosing well at the market

Ripeness at the point of sale is read differently fruit by fruit rather than
by one rule: firm and unbruised for an apple, fragrant and heavy for its
size for citrus, yielding to gentle pressure for an avocado or a papaya, and
— for the fruits sold whole and thick-rinded, such as watermelon — a hollow
sound when thumped rather than anything visible. None of these cues
transfer from one fruit to another; the market decision is made fruit by
fruit, and guessing by a single rule of thumb for all of them is how a
climacteric fruit ends up bought either too green to use this week or
already past the point of scheduling.

## Ripening as a schedulable process

Because a climacteric fruit keeps producing the hormone that ripens it
after harvest, buying one underripe is not a compromise — it is a way of
deciding, rather than discovering, when it will be ready. A firmer,
less-ripe piece bought several days out and left on the counter arrives at
a stated readiness on a stated day; the same fruit bought fully ripe has
already started a clock the buyer does not control. A non-climacteric fruit
offers no such choice: it must be bought at the ripeness it will be eaten
at, because none is coming later.
