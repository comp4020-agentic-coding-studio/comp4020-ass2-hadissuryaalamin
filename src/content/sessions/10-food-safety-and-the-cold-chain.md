Every week since week 2 has stored something: a vegetable, a bird, a fish, a
cut of beef, a piece of fruit. This week does not add a new material. It
takes the storage logic scattered across weeks 2, 3, 4 and 9 and states it
once, as a single account, before the course does anything that requires it
to have already been understood.

## The danger zone

Foodborne bacteria multiply fastest between 4°C and 60°C (40°F to 140°F) —
the range USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service names, plainly, the
danger zone. Inside the middle of that band, some common bacteria can
double in number in as little as twenty minutes. Perishable material —
everything handled since week 2 — must be refrigerated within two hours of
leaving a controlled temperature, or within one hour if the surrounding air
is above 32°C. This is not a new rule; it is the rule week 3 already applied
to poultry and fish, restated as the general case it always was.

## Cross-contamination

The same organisms that make raw poultry, fish and meat perishable transfer
to whatever a knife or a board touches next. The public-health framework
behind this is stated in four steps — clean, separate, cook, chill — and
this week closes out three of them. Clean and separate are the boards,
knives and hands kept apart between raw material and anything eaten as-is;
chill is the refrigeration clock just described. The fourth step, cook, is
the one this course has deliberately not reached yet — it is next week's
subject, not this one's, and naming it here is the clearest way to mark
where the boundary sits.

## How long prepared material keeps

Once refrigerated at or below 4°C, raw poultry and raw ground meats keep
one to two days before they must be cooked or frozen; raw steaks, chops and
roasts keep three to five days under the same conditions. These figures are
the same ones week 3 gave for poultry and fish and week 4's red meat
material follows the same pattern for whole cuts. Cut and ripening fruit
from week 9 sits on a shorter, less uniform clock, set by ripeness rather
than by a fixed rule — which is exactly why week 9 treated ripening as
something to schedule rather than something to simply wait out.

## Why this sits here

Weeks 2, 3, 4 and 9 each taught a storage rule for one kind of material.
Read on their own, they can look like four unrelated pieces of advice — a
shelf here, a temperature there. Read together, they are one argument: a
kitchen's preparation work is only as good as its coldest link, and the
material this course has spent nine weeks handling has been on that clock
the whole time, whether or not it was named.
