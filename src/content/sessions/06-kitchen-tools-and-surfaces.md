## The inventory

A kitchen's non-heat tools fall into a small number of categories, each with
a job distinct from the others: a cutting board to work on, a chopper (knife
or cleaver) to cut with, strainers to separate solid from liquid, and
containers to hold and store what has already been prepared. None of this
week's content is about technique with any of these tools — the knife itself
is week 7's subject — only about the tools and surfaces as objects that need
choosing and maintaining.

## Board material, compared honestly

Wood, plastic and marble each trade off differently across hygiene, knife
wear and particle shedding, and none of the three wins outright.

**Wood** is kind to knife edges — the surface yields slightly rather than
blunting the blade — and, counter to a common assumption, a well-maintained
wood board is not automatically less hygienic than plastic: wood's natural
porosity can draw liquid (and the bacteria in it) below the surface, away
from a cloth or a subsequent cut, though this benefit depends entirely on
the board being dried properly and not left wet. It requires more upkeep
than the other two materials: periodic oiling to prevent cracking, and it
cannot go in a dishwasher.

**Plastic** is dishwasher-safe, cheap to replace, and takes knife scarring
readily — visible cut marks accumulate and, once deep enough, are harder to
clean thoroughly than a smooth surface, which is the usual argument for
replacing a heavily scored plastic board rather than continuing to use it.
It is gentle on edges, similar to wood.

**Marble and other hard stone** boards are the hardest of the three, which
means they are the least forgiving to a knife edge — they dull a blade
faster than wood or plastic — and they shed comparatively little material of
their own to whatever is cut on them, but they are heavy, can chip, and are
mostly chosen for pastry work (where a cold, non-porous surface helps) rather
than as a general-purpose board.

## What cutting actually does to the board — and the food

Every board sheds some material into whatever is chopped on it, an effect
usually invisible and, until recently, not well quantified for plastic. [Yadav
et al. (2023), in *Environmental Science &
Technology*](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/abs/10.1021/acs.est.3c00924), measured
microplastic release from polyethylene and polypropylene cutting boards
during normal chopping and found that a substantial number of microplastic
particles could be generated and transferred to food this way — the [American
Chemical Society's summary of the
study](https://www.acs.org/pressroom/presspacs/2023/june/cutting-boards-can-produce-microparticles-when-chopping-veggies.html)
frames it as cutting boards being an overlooked source of microplastic
exposure through food, not through packaging or water as more commonly
discussed.

Reporting this finding honestly means reporting its limits along with its
headline. The same study tested the effect of the released microplastic
particles on cultured mouse fibroblast cells over 72 hours and found no
significant effect on cell viability at the concentrations tested. The
authors characterised this as an open question rather than a closed one,
and called for further research into the health effects of dietary
microplastic exposure. The correct summary is therefore that plastic boards
measurably shed microplastic into food during use, and that this study did
not find evidence of short-term cell toxicity at the levels it tested — both
halves of that sentence are part of the finding, and dropping either one
misrepresents it.

## Maintenance

Strainers and containers are maintained by the same basic rule as boards:
wash promptly after use, dry fully before storing, and replace anything
whose surface has degraded to the point of trapping food or liquid — a
cracked container lid, a strainer mesh that has started to distort. For a
wood board specifically: wash with hot soapy water rather than soaking,
dry standing on edge so air reaches both faces, and oil periodically with a
food-safe mineral or specialist board oil to keep the wood from drying out
and cracking, which is the main failure mode wood is prone to that plastic
and stone are not.
