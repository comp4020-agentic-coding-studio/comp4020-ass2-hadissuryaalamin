A dish can be described in full before a single ingredient is heated. This
assignment asks for that description: a recipe argued into existence, then
checked against what is already known about the materials involved, rather
than against a stove.

## The brief

> Invent a dish. Write the account of it that a competent cook would need in
> order to reproduce it, and that a nutritionist would need in order to
> assess it, without either of them having seen it made.

The dish is not prepared or cooked for this submission. What is being
assessed is the reasoning: whether the student can predict, from the
ingredients and the process described, what the result will be — in
composition and in the senses.

## What you submit

A written account, structured in three parts.

**Preparation process.** The method as a numbered sequence of steps, each
naming an action, an ingredient and a reason. "Dice the onion finely"
is a step; "dice the onion finely, because a larger surface area shortens
the cooking time and changes how much of its sulfur compounds volatilise"
is the standard this assignment holds itself to.

**Nutritional composition.** A table of the dish's nutritional content —
energy, protein, fat, carbohydrate and any other figure relevant to the
dish's stated purpose — computed from its ingredients. Every figure must
trace to a named food-composition database: USDA FoodData Central or FSANZ
NUTTAB (Australian Food Composition Database) are both acceptable, and a
different national database is acceptable if it is named and cited in the
same way. Cite the specific food entry used for each ingredient, not the
database in general — "chicken breast, raw" is not a citation; "chicken,
broilers or fryers, breast, meat only, raw — FDC ID 171077" is.

**Sensory description.** A prediction of colour, aroma, flavour and texture,
addressed separately. This section is not a marketing paragraph; it is a
claim the student is willing to defend against the finished dish when the
same recipe is re-examined in week 12.

## How it is marked

The `marking` block above renders as a criterion table. In brief: the
preparation process and the nutritional composition carry the most weight,
because they are the parts of the account that can be checked against a
source rather than taken on trust; the sensory description is judged on
precision of language rather than on whether the dish sounds appealing;
sources are marked on whether a reader could locate the exact database
entry cited, not merely on whether a database is named somewhere in the
document.
